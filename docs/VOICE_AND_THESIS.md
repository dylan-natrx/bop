# Voice & Thesis Guide

The north star for every word on the page. Locked with the user (Dylan, PR/comms strategist) on 2026-06-11, during the June data voice pass. Read this before writing or editing any reader-facing copy. It sits on top of the tone rules in [CLAUDE.md](../CLAUDE.md), it does not replace them.

---

## The core thesis (what every section serves)

There is an easy way to choose restoration sites and a right way. The easy way is one formula for the whole harbor and a hope that nature plays along. Nature rarely does. Oysters live where the math says they can't; a healthy harbor changes year to year. So Billion Oyster Project and Natrx did it the harder, more responsible way: a way that leaves room for the people who know these waters and for the fact that nature keeps surprising us.

Underneath the whole piece is the reason oyster reefs are worth caring about at all: **we are building something that is alive.** A reef guards a shoreline and feeds a harbor at the same time. The work is a bet that what we make and what lives around us can do more than share space. They can look after each other.

This rhymes on purpose with the §1 lede ("ecological infrastructure") and Natrx's "Adaptive Infrastructure." Never underline the connection. A savvy reader catches it.

## The capability story (how we prove the science is smart)

Show the judgment in the science, not the vocabulary of it. The proof point is that the framework **adapts**, in two directions that are really one virtue:

- **Across space:** the method travels to any estuary; what it learns is local. New York's answer stays in New York. The way of reaching an answer is what ports.
- **Across time:** it re-runs as new data and understanding arrive. The work is constantly reworked, tested, and informed by the team's own experts. It is interpretive, not a rigid formula that snaps the first time an outlier appears.

The living example is the upper Hudson: oysters persist there in water fresher than a textbook allows, the team's knowledge of that is built into the scoring, and the ranking reflects it. (See `memory/project_methodology_editorial_spine.md` and the June data analysis.)

## Voice principles

1. **Reading level: a curious 11th grader.** Short sentences. Everyday words.
2. **Lead with the human stakes.** Let the data back it up, never the reverse.
3. **Warm and confident, not clinical.** There is a person behind this who cares about the harbor.
4. **Hooks real people can grasp.** Concrete nouns and images over abstractions.
5. **Heart and thesis in every section,** not just facts.

## Retire these techy tells (translate to plain words)

`calibrated` · `tuned` / `tuned for` · `optimize` · `parameters` · `leverage` · `composite` (as a noun) · `robust` · `methodology` (as filler) · `pipeline` (for the site pool; say "candidate sites") · `synthesized` · `footprint` · `built out` · `proof-of-concept` · `go-or-no-go` · `actionable` · `constructability`

These join the existing CLAUDE.md bans: **no em dashes, no "not X, it's Y," no "X is not Y" negation leads, no manufactured drama/surprise/discovery, present tense for the framework and the partnership, past tense for the completed 26-week engagement.**

## Locked, do not rewrite

- **Headline:** "Restoring New York Harbor's Oyster Reefs. *But where?*"
- **Lede** (the "ecological infrastructure" paragraph). Voice-touch only with explicit sign-off; it is already in register.
- **Real human quotes** (Carolyn Khoury, Mike McCann, Lise Montefiore). Never reword what a person said.
- **Score language:** "site score" / "relative site score," never "suitability score," "confidence," or "calibrated HSI."
- **Data-support tiers:** Robust / Strong / Adequate / Limited / Sparse.

## Before / after exemplars (the tuning fork)

**Local knowledge (§3, Step 1):**
> Before: "The optimum is roughly 12 to 20 PSU. The upper Hudson runs too fresh. Sites near Piermont, Irvington, and Ossining drop out at this step."
> After: "Oysters need salt, but not too much and not too little. How much is right depends on the water they came up in. Far up the Hudson, oysters hold on in water fresher than the books say they should. The people who have spent years on this harbor know that, so the science makes room for it. Places like Piermont and Ossining, which a one-size formula would throw out, stay in the running."

**Portability (§5):**
> "The same questions are waiting in San Francisco Bay and the Chesapeake. Where do we start, and how do we know? This way of working can answer them anywhere, because it doesn't bring New York's answer with it. It brings a way of listening to a place: the data a harbor already keeps, and the people who know its water. Every estuary gets its own answer."

**The harder-way beat (§3 intro or §2→§3 bridge):**
> "There is an easy way to do this and a right way. The easy way is one formula for the whole harbor and a hope that nature plays along. It rarely does. Oysters live where the math says they can't. A healthy harbor changes year to year. So we did it the harder way, the one that leaves room for the people who know these waters and for the fact that nature keeps surprising us. That makes the work slower. It also makes it real."

**The close (§5, the harmony idea):**
> "An oyster reef is a hopeful kind of thing: something we build that is also alive. It guards a shoreline and feeds a harbor at the same time. Work like this is a bet that what we make and what lives around us can do more than share space. They can look after each other."
