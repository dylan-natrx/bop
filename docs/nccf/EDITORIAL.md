## The spine

**A coast that everyone knew was eroding could finally be measured, and the measurement showed the loss was concentrated in a small fraction of it.**

Every section serves that sentence. If a section does not advance it, the section is wrong. It never appears on the page in those words.

Note what it does not say. It does not say a crisis was discovered. Jacob Boyd, who has lived in Eastern North Carolina his whole life and runs the Federation's salt marsh program, was asked directly whether the results moved any site up or down his list. He said no. He knew where the erosion was. What he could not do was prove it, fund it, or permit it.

The finding is the shape of the loss, not its existence.

---

## Why this is the spine

Three findings stack under it.

**Erosion is concentrated.** The fastest 10% of eroding locations account for 43.5% of all land lost, computed across roughly 76,000 eroding transect points. This is a heavy-tailed distribution. Most of the coast is losing ground slowly. A small fraction is disappearing.

**The measurement did not exist before.** Not because anyone was negligent, but because a survey at this resolution across this footprint was never within reach of a coastal program's budget. Jacob's prior method was field verification, site by site, before a candidate list even existed.

**The cheap wide screen aimed the expensive precise one.** Satellite wetland change, mapped across the region, carries enough signal to say where the detailed survey is warranted. Not enough to replace it. Enough to aim it.

Together they make an argument a state coastal budget can act on.

## The lead is economic

The story travels to a business desk and requires nobody to believe a carbon number.

Better data means smaller builds. Living shorelines sized to the wave conditions actually measured at a site rather than to a default design. Less tonnage, less cost, public money spent where it does the most.

The client says this himself, on record:

> "Depending on how much erosion is happening, what determines the size of living shoreline you build or type of living shoreline. It's all based off of the better data you have. The better design you'll be able to do and really the most cost effective, because there are some sites where historically people have just built some living shorelines and stuff up to what the permit would say, even though it may not actually call for that in some of these areas."

Building to what the permit allows rather than to what the site needs is the practice this challenges. That is the conflict, and it comes from the client rather than from us.

His account of the resistance is milder than ours and is the version that gets quoted:

> "People are used to doing things the way they're used to doing them. Any sort of change, for good or bad, always takes a little bit of getting people to rethink the way they approach things."

**Do not put sharper framing in Jacob's mouth.** "Surgical," the added-tonnage anecdote, and "challenges entrenched practice" were Dylan's words in the interview. Jacob agreed in general terms only.

## Carbon

**Decision, locked: carbon appears once, as the reason the grant exists, and nowhere else.**

What the page may say. The federal grant behind this work was aimed at keeping carbon in coastal soil. NCCF targeted sites for carbon rather than treating it as a side benefit, which is a departure from how this work is usually funded. The program set out to protect up to 600 acres of marsh from eroding. Natrx's contribution was finding and ranking the erosion.

Jacob's framing, quotable:

> "It's a lot different than some of our previous work where the carbon was really the co-benefit. This was really targeting these areas for carbon."

What the page may not say, under any circumstance:

- That Natrx measured, quantified, modeled, or credited carbon. It did not. The deliverable was erosion-based site selection.
- Verra, REDD+, VM0007, or VM0033. Nick, 2026-07-22: "I'd be very wary of mentioning Verra at all."
- Any CO2e tonnage figure, including the SOW's 183,000 Mt and 305 Mt per acre. Acres are a land-protection target. Tonnage is a carbon-accounting claim and invites the methodology fight we are not having.
- The global percentage (Temmink et al. 2022, 1.9 Gt, ~4.5%). Real and citable, but it belongs to a different Natrx thesis and pulls the page toward a macro carbon argument.
- Jacob's per-acre NOAA sequestration rate, until he supplies the citation. He could not name it on the call.

The whole carbon section is one paragraph. If it runs longer, it has become an argument.

## The claim that must not be overstated

**Wetland change is a triage signal, not a predictor.**

The correlation is real and highly significant (Spearman rho = 0.349, p < 10^-39, n = 1,354). It is also weak in absolute terms (Pearson r squared = 0.038, under 4% of variance in shoreline change).

Both are true. Publishing the first without the second is the overclaim that gets the page taken apart by a science reporter.

**Say:** the wetland signal is strong enough to rank locations and target where surveys go.
**Do not say:** we can predict shoreline erosion from satellite imagery.
**Do not say:** the analysis is validated, calibrated, or proven, without saying what was validated and how.

A Phase I screen is not supposed to predict. It is supposed to aim. The honest claim is the more useful one.

## The two-phase method is the product

**Phase I:** broad, regional, remote, far less costly. Wetland change analysis across a coastline. Finds the hotspots.
**Phase II:** intensive, targeted, precise. Shoreline change analysis where Phase I says it is warranted.

The NCCF engagement produced both, which is what makes this a demonstration rather than an assertion. Under the original scope it would have produced Phase II in a limited area only. The expanded coverage is what made the comparison possible.

Nick's framing of the expansion as "over-delivery" is a filter, not a ceiling. The over-delivery is the finding.

## The horizon, handled carefully

Above the finding and the method sits the reason this matters beyond one grant in one state: a category of coastline that could not be measured at scale now can be, efficiently enough to act on, while the land-to-water problem accelerates.

Three rules keep that honest.

**State the fact, earn the feeling.** The page can say plainly that no comparable dataset existed. Nick, who built it, cannot point to one. Stating that is reporting. The felt scale comes from walking one coast and then pulling the camera back, not from asserting significance.

**Demonstrate the capability, do not proclaim it.** Show what was never realistic: Nick's $3.5 to $5 million estimate for the traditional approach, the practical ceiling of field inspection at "less than a single municipality," the two people in the company who can do the interpretation. The reader concludes it is rare because the evidence says so.

**Keep the claim about capability and need, not market position.** What is now possible, and why the need grows, is the page's voice. Natrx's commercial standing stays off the page or lives in Nick's attributed words.

## Register

New York Times feature voice. Concrete nouns and verbs. Comfortable with a long sentence when it is earned. Past tense for completed work.

**Hard rules:**
- No em dashes.
- No "it's not X, it's Y" or "it's not just X, it's Y."
- No AI clichés, no rhetorical parallel constructions.
- Lead with what things are.
- *Natrx Assess* italicized as a named capability. Natrx never rendered "Matrix," which is how both transcripts auto-render it.

**Scientific language:**
- Statistical terms carry statistical meanings. If the page uses "significant," it explains that significance and strength are different things.
- Uncertainty is stated, not smoothed.
- Reading level: 11th grade. A short glossary for the terms that survive (transect, accretion, one-meter resolution).

## What the page is for

**Journalists** want the erosion finding and the concentration number. That lands without any methodology.

**Coastal program managers in other states** want the method. Statewide screening, then targeted survey where it matters. That produces the next contract.

Write for the first. The second reads it anyway. A page written for procurement does not get covered, and a page that gets covered gets read by procurement.

Jacob's own audience note, which the page should honor: the "football fields lost" framing is exhausted. He asked for fresh numbers tied to insurance and tourism, and for coverage that arrives before the next storm rather than after it.

## Correction, 2026-09-02: the screening-to-survey sequence

**An earlier rule in this file said "the wide screen did not select the 39 survey areas." That
overstates the case and is withdrawn.** It was built from interview fragments and was never checked
against the contract. The SOW says the opposite of what we were enforcing.

**Phase II, Wetlands Assessment & Change Analysis:**

> "Natrx will conduct a natural asset inventory of NC's flooded vegetation and a change analysis
> within the region outlined in Figure 1 **to best understand where further analysis is warranted.**
> The change analysis will highlight wetland loss hotspots, and **identify areas addressable for the
> Phase III erosion and carbon analysis.**"

**Phase III, Erosion & Carbon Analysis:**

> "**Once addressable areas are identified during Phase II**, Natrx will conduct spatial analysis to
> model carbon distribution in combination with erosion rates for those areas in order to prioritize
> opportunity sites."

So the wide pass came first and its contracted job was to identify the areas the granular pass would
measure. Nick's own framing matches: *"you can be more intelligent about where you exercise your
shoreline change analysis budget"* (2026-07-22, 13:06).

**What is true, and what the page may say.** The change analysis identified the addressable areas.
The final site list was settled with the Federation, per the SOW's Phase IV: *"The final selection of
shoreline segments will be determined in coordination with NCCF."* And coverage then expanded far
beyond the plan, 25 subprojects becoming 39, to the point where Nick jokes the screening phase was
almost redundant.

**Publishable:** the first pass pointed to the areas worth measuring, and the final list was settled
with the Coastal Federation. Both halves, together.

**Not publishable:** that the screen alone picked the sites, with no client role. And still not
publishable, unchanged: any claim that the wide pass *predicts* erosion. The r² of 0.038 governs that
separately and is untouched by this correction.

**Cost of the error.** This rule was enforced through three editorial sessions and shaped the
interactive twice. Check the contract before writing a rule from an interview.
